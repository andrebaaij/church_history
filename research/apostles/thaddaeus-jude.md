# Thaddaeus / Jude ("Judas of James")

One of the Twelve has two names in the Gospels. Matthew and Mark call him **Thaddaeus**; Luke and Acts call him **Judas "of James"**; and John calls him "Judas (not Iscariot)" when he asks Jesus a question at the Last Supper. The New Testament says nothing about him after Pentecost. Later stories send him to Edessa (in modern Turkey), where King Abgar is said to have been healed, then to Armenia, or to Persia together with Simon the Zealot, and they place his death in Armenia, Persia or Beirut. These stories come from the 4th century and later, and they often mix him up with other men named Thaddaeus or Jude. One example is Thaddaeus/Addai, "one of the Seventy". Another is Jude, the brother of the Lord, who wrote the Letter of Jude.

## Competing traditions

### Disambiguation: three or four "Judes/Thaddaeuses"
1. **Thaddaeus = "Judas of James" = "Judas not Iscariot", one of the Twelve.**
   - Matthew 10:3 and Mark 3:18 have "Thaddaeus" in the list where Luke 6:16 and Acts 1:13 have "Judas of James". Harmonising the lists gives the traditional identification: the lists line up name for name, though this is an inference (A for the lists, inference unmarked).
   - Textual variant: at Matt 10:3 the Western text (Codex Bezae, D) reads **"Lebbaeus"**. The Byzantine/KJV text reads "Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus". Sinaiticus and Vaticanus read "Thaddaeus" only, and the UBS committee (Metzger, *Textual Commentary*) prefers "Thaddaeus".
   - The Greek *Ioudas Iakōbou* (Luke 6:16) is literally "Judas of James". Modern translations (ESV, NIV) read "son of James"; the KJV reads "brother of James".
2. **Jude, brother of the Lord** (Mark 6:3; Matt 13:55), traditionally the author of the Letter of Jude ("brother of James", Jude 1). Acts 1:13–14 lists the apostles and then "his brothers" separately, and John 7:5 says the brothers did not believe during the ministry. For these reasons many Protestant and modern scholars distinguish him from the apostle. Catholic tradition has often identified them (e.g. Catholic Encyclopedia, "Epistle of St. Jude", 1910). Jude 17 ("remember the words spoken beforehand by the apostles") is often read as the author not counting himself among the apostles. **No winner is picked here.** Hegesippus' grandsons story (row 11) belongs to this Jude.
3. **Thaddaeus / Addai of Edessa, "one of the Seventy"** (Eusebius *HE* 1.13; *Doctrine of Addai*). Eusebius calls him "an apostle, one of the Seventy" (1.13.10), using "apostle" in a broad sense. Pseudo-Hippolytus' *Seventy* list also has "Thaddeus, who conveyed the epistle to Augarus" as a separate person from the apostle Jude/Lebbaeus. Later texts (Greek *Acts of Thaddaeus*, the Golden Legend) merge Addai with the apostle.
4. Also confused with him: **Simon the Zealot = "son of Clopas, also called Jude"** (Pseudo-Hippolytus §11). This is a merger with Symeon son of Clopas, bishop of Jerusalem (Eusebius *HE* 3.11, 3.32). It is covered in simon-zealot.md.

### Tradition A — Edessa (King Abgar)
- **Claim:** Thaddaeus heals and converts Abgar V Ukkama of Edessa after the Ascension.
- **Earliest source:** Eusebius *HE* 1.13 (c. 311–325), who says he translated it from Syriac archives. The Syriac *Doctrine of Addai* (c. 400) is a fuller version.
- **Grade:** C for "Eusebius found an Edessene document". The events themselves rest on that document.
- **Later sources:** Egeria's travel diary (Edessa, 384) mentions the Abgar letter (**UNVERIFIED** for an exact reference); the Greek *Acts of Thaddaeus*; the Golden Legend ch. 159 ("Jude was sent by Thomas to the king Abgarus of Edessa").
- **Notes:** The person is "one of the Seventy" in the earliest sources. Scholars widely treat the correspondence as legendary and date the formal conversion of Edessa's royal house much later (Bauer, *Orthodoxy and Heresy*, ch. 1; Schaff-Herzog s.v. "Abgar").

### Tradition B — Armenia (Artaz)
- **Claim:** Thaddaeus evangelises Armenia and is martyred under King Sanatruk in Shavarshan/Artaz. His tomb is at the Monastery of St Thaddeus (Qara Kelisa), NW Iran. Thaddaeus and Bartholomew are the "First Enlighteners" of Armenia.
- **Earliest source:** Movses Khorenatsi, *History of Armenia* 2.33–34. The date is disputed: the traditional date is 5th c., but many scholars place it in the 8th c. A separate Armenian *Martyrdom of Thaddaeus* exists (NASSCAL e-Clavis entry), but its date is **UNVERIFIED**.
- **Grade:** D/E.
- **Later evidence:** The monastery (present church largely 13th–19th c.) was inscribed in 2008 as part of UNESCO WHS 1262, "Armenian Monastic Ensembles of Iran", as a place of pilgrimage of the Apostle Thaddeus. The traditional martyrdom date of AD 66 is repeated in popular sources and has no ancient basis.

### Tradition C — Persia with Simon the Zealot (Suanir)
- **Claim:** Jude preaches in Mesopotamia/Pontus and Simon in Egypt. They meet in Persia and are martyred together at Suanir.
- **Earliest source:** Latin *Passio Simonis et Iudae* in the Pseudo-Abdias *Virtutes apostolorum* collection. It is attributed to "Abdias, bishop of Babylon", which is fictitious. Manuscripts date from the 8th century onward, and the composition date is uncertain (c. 6th c.? **UNVERIFIED**).
- **Grade:** D.
- **Later sources:** Golden Legend ch. 159 (city "Suamar"). This tradition explains why the Western calendar keeps a joint feast of Simon and Jude on 28 October.

### Tradition D — Peaceful death at Beirut (Berytus)
- **Claim:** He preached in Edessa, Mesopotamia and Syria and died (not martyred) at Berytus in Phoenicia.
- **Sources:** Greek *Acts of Thaddaeus*; Pseudo-Hippolytus *On the Twelve* §10.
- **Grade:** D.
- The Latin *Breviarium* has "Beirut, a city in Armenia". This looks like a garbled version of the same tradition.

## Open questions / uncertainties

- **Identity:** It is unproven whether the Thaddaeus of Edessa (Eusebius: "one of the Seventy") is the apostle. The earliest sources say no, and later legend says yes. The map should not draw the apostle at Edessa without this caveat.
- **Jude the brother of the Lord vs Jude the apostle:** Scholars and churches disagree. Hegesippus' Domitian story (and Vreugdenhil ch. 7) concerns the Lord's brother's family. It should not appear on the apostle's track unless the teacher accepts that identification.
- **Edessa date:** Eusebius' year 340 of the Seleucid era is about AD 28/29 (Eusebius himself equates it to about AD 30), and the *Doctrine of Addai* has 343 (≈ AD 31/32). These dates sit awkwardly with an Ascension of AD 30 or 33, and they are not historical anchors.
- **Armenian martyrdom date (AD 66 / AD 50):** Both are modern church tradition. No ancient source gives them.
- **Suanir:** The location is unknown. The Golden Legend spells it "Suamar", and attempts to identify it are speculative.
- Names of Jude's grandsons ("Zoker and James", reportedly from a Philip of Side fragment quoting Hegesippus): **UNVERIFIED**, so not used.
- Egeria's reference to the Abgar letter and the date of the Armenian *Martyrdom of Thaddaeus*: **UNVERIFIED**.
- Vreugdenhil gives no source for "Thaddeus … died the martyrs death". It is consistent with Traditions B and C but not with the Beirut/Edessa peaceful-death accounts.
