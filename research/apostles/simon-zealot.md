# Simon the Zealot (Simon the Cananaean)

Simon was one of the twelve apostles Jesus chose. The Gospels call him "the Zealot" (Luke, Acts) or "the Cananaean" (Matthew, Mark), and the two names mean the same thing: "the zealous one". Apart from appearing in the lists of the Twelve and praying with them in Jerusalem after the Ascension, the Bible says nothing more about him. Later Christians told very different stories about where he went: Egypt and North Africa, Britain, Persia (together with Jude), or the Black Sea coast in the Caucasus. None of these stories goes back further than several centuries after his death. Vreugdenhil only says that he "died the martyrs death".

## Competing traditions

### Disambiguation first — three or four "Simons"
- **Simon the Zealot / Cananaean** (Matt 10:4; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13) — the apostle. Grade A for his existence and place among the Twelve; nothing else in the NT.
- **Simon the brother of the Lord** (Mark 6:3; Matt 13:55) — one of Jesus' brothers. In the Gospels Jesus' brothers did not believe in him during his ministry (John 7:5), which makes identifying this Simon with one of the Twelve difficult; the NT never identifies them. The *Golden Legend* nevertheless makes Simon and Jude "brethren of James the Less and sons of Mary Cleophas" (D; follows the medieval Western harmonisation).
- **Symeon son of Clopas**, second bishop of Jerusalem. Hegesippus (c. 180, quoted by Eusebius) says Clopas was Joseph's brother, that Symeon was chosen bishop after James' death (Eusebius HE 3.11), and that he was crucified under Trajan when Atticus was governor, aged 120 (HE 3.32). **Grade B for Symeon** — but nothing in Hegesippus/Eusebius identifies him with the apostle Simon.
- **The conflation**: Pseudo-Hippolytus, *On the Twelve* §11, writes "Simon the Zealot, the son of Clopas, who is also called Jude, became bishop of Jerusalem after James the Just, and fell asleep and was buried there at the age of 120 years." The *Golden Legend* repeats the Jerusalem-bishop story ("it is founden in divers places, of S. Simon, that he was nailed to the cross") but immediately adds: "some say verily that it was not this Simon that suffered the martyrdom of the cross, but it was another, the son of Cleophas, brother of Joseph", citing Eusebius' chronicle, Bede's retractions and Usuard. **Teachers: do not map Simon the Zealot to Jerusalem c. 107 as fact.**

### The names "Zealot" and "Cananaean"
- *Kananaios* (Matt 10:4; Mark 3:18) transliterates Aramaic *qan'an*, "zealous" (from Hebrew/Aramaic *qn'*); Luke translates it with Greek *Zēlōtēs* (Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). So it is **not** "Canaanite" (KJV) and most modern scholars do not take it as "from Cana". Source: Strong's G2581 / HELPS (Bible Hub) — a popular lexicon; BDAG s.v. Καναναῖος gives the same (BDAG not consulted online — UNVERIFIED).
- **Older reading "of Cana"**: the *Golden Legend* explains "Simon Cananean of Cana a street that is in Galilee, there whereas our Lord converted the water into wine". Wikipedia attributes this etymology to Jerome and Bede (UNVERIFIED — exact passages not located). Byzantine/Orthodox tradition makes Simon the bridegroom at the wedding at Cana (John 2) — E; no early source located.
- **Was he a member of the Zealot party?** The faction that called itself "Zealots" is first attested by Josephus during the Jewish War, 67–68: "for that was the name they went by, as if they were zealous in good undertakings" (Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.3.9 [§160–161 Niese]). Many scholars (e.g. R. Horsley, *Bandits, Prophets, and Messiahs*, 1985) argue there was no organized "Zealot party" as early as c. 30, so "zealot" may describe Simon's religious zeal (cf. Gal 1:14; Acts 21:20, 22:3, where "zealous" is used of observant Jews) rather than membership of a revolutionary movement. Others (M. Hengel, *Die Zeloten*, 1961) see a continuous zealot movement from Judas the Galilean (AD 6) onward. Not resolved; do not present Simon as a "freedom fighter" as fact. (Scholarly positions summarised from Wikipedia "Zealots (Judea)"; Horsley/Hengel not consulted directly — UNVERIFIED in detail.)

### Tradition A — Egypt, then Persia with Jude, martyred there
- **Claim:** Simon preached in Egypt, Jude in Mesopotamia; they met in Persia, defeated the magicians Zaroes and Arphaxat (whom "S. Matthew had driven out of Ethiopia"), baptised a king and 60,000 men, and after a long mission were killed by pagan priests at "Suamar"/"Suanir" when they refused to sacrifice to the sun and moon; lightning destroyed the temple. Buried in Babylon (*Passio*) or in the king's city (*Golden Legend*).
- **Earliest source:** *Passio Simonis et Iudae*, a Latin text in the Pseudo-Abdias collection (*Virtutes apostolorum*); it claims to derive from "Abdias, bishop of Babylon", translated by "Eutropius"/"Africanus" — a literary fiction. Earliest manuscript c. 800 (Montpellier). Composition date not established in the source I checked (NASSCAL e-Clavis, T. Burke, 2023). **Grade D.**
- **Later:** Jacobus de Voragine, *Golden Legend* (c. 1260), "SS. Simon and Jude" — verified; it does **not** describe Simon being sawn. The **saw** as Simon's attribute (very common in Western art) and the claim that he was "sawn in half at Suanir" are attributed by Wikipedia to Justus Lipsius (16th c.) — UNVERIFIED; no ancient text for the sawing was found in this research.
- Western liturgical consequence: Simon and Jude share a feast on 28 October (NASSCAL; Catholic Encyclopedia).

### Tradition B — North Africa (Mauretania) and Britain
- **Claim:** Simon preached "through all Mauretania and Afric the less" and in Britain, where he was crucified and buried.
- **Earliest source:** Pseudo-Dorotheus of Tyre, *Synopsis* / lists of apostles. Attributed to Dorotheus, bishop of Tyre (d. c. 362), but not by him; Wikipedia (Pseudo-Dorotheus) calls them "late 3rd-century… pseudepigraphically attributed" works, while the standard view (T. Schermann, *Prophetarum vitae fabulosae*, 1907) dates the Greek apostle lists much later, roughly 6th–8th c. — dating UNVERIFIED. The Simon/Britain text itself was not located online — UNVERIFIED. **Grade D.**
- **Later:** Nicephorus Callistus Xanthopoulos (14th c.), *Ecclesiastical History* 2.40 — commonly cited for Simon preaching in Egypt, Cyrene, Africa, Mauretania, Libya and "the British Isles"; I could not open the text, so the book.chapter is UNVERIFIED. (Wikipedia attributes the same quote to "Nikephoros I of Constantinople" (9th c.) — probably a confusion of the two Nicephori.) John Foxe, *Acts and Monuments* (1563), repeats it (verified, see Timeline row 6). Also reported by Holinshed's *Chronicles* (1587) side-notes (could not open the Oxford Holinshed site — UNVERIFIED).
- **Modern elaborations (E, no ancient source):** two visits to Britain (AD 44 and 60) and crucifixion at Caistor, Lincolnshire, on 10 May 61 under Catus Decianus. These come from 19th–20th-c. British-Israel/"Glastonbury" literature (e.g. G. F. Jowett, *The Drama of the Lost Disciples*, 1961 — UNVERIFIED). Wikipedia cites Baronius and "Hippolytus" for this, but the Pseudo-Hippolytus text (verified) puts Simon in **Jerusalem**, not Britain.
- **Note:** Catholic Encyclopedia (Löffler, 1912): for Simon's preaching "almost all the lands of the then known world, even as far as Britain, have been mentioned".

### Tradition C — Caucasus / Black Sea (Abkhazia, Nikopsis, Iberia)
- **Claim:** Simon (sometimes with Andrew) preached on the eastern Black Sea coast and was buried at Nikopsis; later cult at Anakopia (New Athos, Abkhazia), where a church of St Simon the Canaanite (9th–10th c., rebuilt 1880s) stands on the Psyrtskha stream and a grotto is shown as his hermitage.
- **Earliest source:** Epiphanius the Monk, *Life of Andrew* (c. 820, revised before 843): saw at Nikopsis a tomb with relics inscribed "of Simon the Canaanite". George the Hagiorite (11th c.): Simon was buried "in our land, in Abkhazia, at the place which is called Nikopsi". Both via Wikipedia "Nicopsis" citing C. Mango (2002) and Z. Anchabadze (1959) — primary texts not consulted (UNVERIFIED in detail). **Grade E** (local tradition, first written ~750 years later).
- **Related:** Movses Khorenatsi 2.34 (verified): "as for Simon, who drew Persia as his lot, I can say nothing for certain about what he did or where he was martyred. It is narrated by some that a certain apostle Simon was martyred in Veriosp'or; but whether this is true … I do not know." Veriosp'or is located in Caucasian Iberia/among the Alans by some modern writers — location UNVERIFIED. Movses' own date is disputed (traditionally 5th c.; many modern scholars 8th c.). **Grade C**, explicitly hedged by the author.
- The tomb location: Nikopsis is unidentified — hypotheses include Novomikhaylovsky near Tuapse (Russia) or Anakopia/New Athos (Abkhazia) (Vinogradov 2014, via Wikipedia).
- The grotto at New Athos: popular/local tradition only — E; no written source found.

### Tradition D — Jerusalem bishop, crucified under Trajan
See Disambiguation above: Pseudo-Hippolytus (D) and the *Golden Legend* (which corrects it) apply Symeon son of Clopas' martyrdom (B, Hegesippus via Eusebius HE 3.32) to Simon the Zealot. The Latin *Breviarium apostolorum* (earliest MS 8th c.; verified via NASSCAL translation) combines both: Simon "received the lordship of Egypt and is said to have been the next bishop of Jerusalem, following James the Just. After a hundred and twenty years he was found worthy to receive a martyr's passion and was crucified under Hadrian. He lies in the Bosphorus" — note "Hadrian" instead of Hegesippus' Trajan, and a burial "in the Bosphorus" (unexplained; perhaps related to the Black Sea tradition). Foxe, citing Dorotheus, has "Simon called Cananeus … was bishop of Jerusalem after James, and was crucified in a city of Egypt in the time of Trajanus" — a further variant.

### Tradition E — other (unverified)
- Peaceful death at Edessa — listed in Wikipedia without a source; UNVERIFIED, E.
- Ethiopian tradition that he was crucified in Samaria — Wikipedia, no source; UNVERIFIED, E.
- Origen's list of the apostles' "lots" (Eusebius HE 3.1) does **not** mention Simon (verified) — i.e. the earliest surviving distribution tradition knows nothing about him.

## Open questions / uncertainties
1. **Nothing after Acts 1:13 is historically secure.** All mission/death traditions are D or E and contradict each other (Persia vs Britain vs Caucasus vs Jerusalem). For the map, consider showing them as alternative, clearly labelled legends.
2. **Simon vs Symeon son of Clopas**: the Jerusalem/Trajan (or, in the *Breviarium*, Hadrian) tradition is almost certainly a conflation; Symeon himself (Grade B) belongs in a James/Jerusalem-church file, not here.
3. **Pseudo-Dorotheus date** — "late 3rd c." (Wikipedia) vs 6th–8th c. (Schermann tradition). Needs checking in a scholarly source before teachers rely on the Britain tradition.
4. **Nicephorus Callistus HE 2.40** — reference widely repeated but not verified in PG 145; and possibly confused with Nikephoros I.
5. **Sawing**: no ancient text found; seems iconographic/early-modern. Verify Lipsius reference.
6. **Isidore's Simon entry** — reported as Egypt + Jerusalem bishop; Latin text not checked.
7. **"Zealot" meaning** — zeal for the Law vs revolutionary party: open scholarly debate.
8. **Vreugdenhil** gives no source for "died the martyrs death"; closest match is Foxe ("he was likewise crucified").
9. Locations "Suanir", "Veriosp'or" and Nikopsis are unidentified; no coordinates should be shown as precise.
