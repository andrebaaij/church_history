# James son of Zebedee ("James the Greater")

Summary (youth group): James was a fisherman from Galilee, the brother of John. Jesus
called the two brothers "Boanerges", sons of thunder. With Peter and John he belonged
to the inner circle that saw the transfiguration and was near Jesus in Gethsemane. Jesus
told James and John they would drink his cup (Mark 10:39), and James was the first of
the Twelve to die for Christ: King Herod Agrippa I had him killed with the sword in
Jerusalem, probably in AD 44 (Acts 12:2). Much later, from the 7th century onward,
Christians in Spain came to believe he had preached there, and from the 9th century
that his body lay at Santiago de Compostela — a tradition historians regard as very
unlikely.

> **Do not confuse** with James the brother of the Lord (`james-brother-of-jesus.md`),
> who led the Jerusalem church and was killed in 62. Vreugdenhil ch. 1 tells the death
> of "the apostle James" thrown from the temple — that is the *brother of the Lord's*
> story (Hegesippus), not this James.

## Competing traditions

### Tradition 1 — James stayed in Jerusalem/Judea and died there in 44
- Claim: James is only ever found in Jerusalem after the Ascension and is killed there.
- Earliest source: Acts 1:13; 12:1–2. Grade A.
- Later sources: Clement of Alexandria via Eusebius *HE* 2.9 (B); Jerome, *De vir.
  ill.* 9 ("James, the apostle whom Herod, after our Lord's passion, beheaded") (C).
- Note: Acts gives no movements of James between 1:13 and 12:2. Paul's visit c. 35–37
  (Gal 1:18–19) mentions only Peter and James the Lord's brother.

### Tradition 2 — James preached in Spain (Hispania)
- Claim: James preached in Spain / "the western places" before returning to Jerusalem
  to die.
- Earliest source: *Breviarium Apostolorum*, a Latin list of apostles' mission fields,
  late 6th or 7th c., probably written in southern Gaul or northern Italy: "Hic
  [Iacobus] Hispaniae occidentalia loca predicat" (as quoted by Xacopedia; exact Latin
  wording of the critical edition UNVERIFIED). It does **not** place his burial in Spain
  (burial at "Achaia Marmarica", a disputed name). Grade D.
- Later sources:
  - Pseudo-Isidore, *De ortu et obitu patrum* (interpolated passage, before c. 750;
    attribution to Isidore of Seville generally rejected): James preached "to the
    peoples of Spain and the western places, at the world's edge" — but was not buried
    there (Fletcher's translation). Chapter number in PL 83 UNVERIFIED. Grade D.
  - Aldhelm of Malmesbury, *Carmina ecclesiastica* 4.4 (c. 670–710): "Primitus Hispanas
    convertit dogmate gentes" — "St James was the first to convert the Spanish peoples
    with his teaching" (Lapidge & Rosier tr., 1985, p. 52). Grade D.
  - Hymn *O Dei verbum* (783–788; acrostic names King Mauregatus; attribution to Beatus
    of Liébana disputed): calls James golden head of Hispania and its patron. Grade D/E.
- Counter-evidence (not a separate tradition but relevant):
  - Julian of Toledo (c. 686) used the *Breviarium* yet ignored the Spanish link
    (Fletcher, following Duchesne; Julian's primary passage UNVERIFIED).
  - Paul, Romans 15:20, 24 (c. 57): plans to go to Spain and says he does not build on
    another man's foundation — cited by the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1910) as a
    difficulty.
  - Acts 12:2: death in 44 leaves little room for a Spanish mission.
- Scholarly verdict (reference, not graded): Fletcher (1984): "the story is in the
  highest degree unlikely"; following Duchesne, "there is no sign whatsoever that during
  the first six centuries … anyone believed that St. James had either preached or been
  buried in Spain." *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1910): "numerous scholars reject it. The
  Bollandists however defended it."

### Tradition 3 — James's body at Santiago de Compostela
- Claim: after his death his disciples carried his body by boat to Galicia, where it
  was buried; the tomb was rediscovered in the 9th century.
- Earliest sources:
  - Discovery: under Bishop Theodemir of Iria (d. 847; his tomb slab was found in the
    cathedral excavations of 1946–59) and King Alfonso II (791–842); Fletcher dates it
    between 818 and 842. Earliest narrative: *Concordia de Antealtares* (1077), naming
    the hermit Pelagius (Paio). Grade E for the identification of the tomb.
  - Belief in the relics in Spain is first attested outside Spain by Usuard's
    *Martyrology* (c. 865): relics "translated from Jerusalem to Spain and deposited in
    its uttermost region" (Fletcher's translation). Grade D/E.
  - Translation legend: *Epistola Leonis* (apocryphal "letter of Pope Leo"; dated
    variously 9th to early 11th c.): seven disciples sail seven days to Iria where the
    Sar and Ulla meet. The disciples Athanasius and Theodore first appear in the *Codex
    Calixtinus* bk 3 (mid-12th c.) as guardians of the tomb. Grade D.
- Notes: the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1910) records that the authenticity of the relics
  "has been questioned and is still doubted", while Pope Leo XIII's bull *Omnipotens
  Deus* (1884) affirmed it. This is a living devotional tradition (Camino de Santiago);
  present it as tradition, not history.

### Tradition 4 — Our Lady of the Pillar (Zaragoza, AD 40)
- Claim: Mary appeared to James at Caesaraugusta (Zaragoza) on 2 January 40 while he
  was preaching in Spain.
- Earliest written source: a 1297 note in a codex in the Pilar archive (per a
  popular source: https://fsspx.news/es/news/nuestra-senora-del-pilar-patrona-la-hispanidad-12-octubre-22702); devotion "cannot be documented in Zaragoza
  itself before the 13th century" (Silanes Susaeta, dialnet article). Grade E.
  Primary documents UNVERIFIED.

## Death / martyrdom

### Account 1 — Beheaded by Herod Agrippa I in Jerusalem (c. 44)
- Earliest source: Acts 12:1–2 ("he killed James the brother of John with the sword").
  Grade A.
- Date basis: the episode is set under "Herod the king" = Agrippa I, who ruled all
  Judea 41–44; Acts 12:20–23 then narrates his death at Caesarea, which Josephus
  (*Ant.* 19.8.2) places in the 3rd/4th year of Claudius' reign over Judea, i.e. 44.
  Peter's arrest at Passover (Acts 12:3–4) suggests spring, probably 44 (some allow
  41–43). Range: **41–44, most likely 44.**
- "With the sword" is usually understood as beheading; Clement (via Eusebius *HE*
  2.9.3) says "beheaded".
- Fulfils Mark 10:39 ("the cup that I drink you will drink").

### Account 2 — Clement's detail: the converted accuser
- Clement of Alexandria, *Hypotyposes* bk 7 (c. 200), quoted by Eusebius *HE* 2.9.2–3:
  the accuser confessed Christ, asked forgiveness, James said "Peace be with you" and
  kissed him, "and thus they were both beheaded at the same time". Grade B (Clement
  says he received it "from those who had lived before him").

### Account 3 — Killed together with John "by the Jews" (Papias fragment)
- A 5th-c. epitome attributed to Philip of Side, and George Hamartolos (9th c.), say
  Papias (bk 2) reported that "John the theologian and James his brother were killed by
  the Jews". Grade C. Mostly relevant to John's death; see `john.md` (Death, Account 3).
  Does not contradict Acts 12:2 for James.

## Open questions / uncertainties

1. **Vreugdenhil conflation**: ch. 1 tells the death of "the apostle James" by being
   pushed from the temple roof, stoned, and clubbed — this is Hegesippus' story of James
   the brother of the Lord (Eusebius *HE* 2.23). Vreugdenhil does not narrate Acts 12:2.
   Clement (in Eusebius *HE* 2.1.4) already distinguished "two Jameses: one called the
   Just, who was thrown from the pinnacle… and another who was beheaded."
2. **Exact year** of death: 41–44; 44 is the usual choice because Acts 12:20–23 places
   Agrippa's death right after and Josephus dates that to 44. Acts 12:1 "about that
   time" is loose.
3. **Spain/Santiago**: no source before the late 6th/7th century; tomb identification
   9th century. Show on the map only as a later tradition (grade D/E), if at all.
4. Some Latin primary texts for the Spanish tradition (Breviarium, De ortu, O Dei
   verbum, Julian of Toledo) were seen only through secondary sources — marked
   UNVERIFIED above.
