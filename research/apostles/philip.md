# Philip (the apostle)

Philip came from Bethsaida, the fishing town of Andrew and Peter, and was one of the first
disciples Jesus called. He brought Nathanael to Jesus. John's Gospel shows him at several key
moments: the feeding of the five thousand, bringing Greeks to Jesus, and asking "show us the
Father". After Acts 1:13 the New Testament does not mention the apostle again. Christian writers
from about AD 190 say he died and was buried at Hierapolis in Phrygia (near modern Pamukkale,
Turkey), and the remains of a large 5th-century pilgrimage church built for him survive there.
A complication runs through the whole story: Acts also has a *different* Philip, "the
evangelist", one of the Seven (Acts 6:5), who had four prophesying daughters (Acts 21:8–9), and
the early writers seem to have mixed up the two.

> **Read this first — two Philips.** Rows marked **[EVANGELIST]** are about Philip the
> evangelist/deacon, who in the New Testament is a *different person* from the apostle (the
> Twelve call the Seven in Acts 6:2–5; Peter and John, not Philip, are sent to Samaria in Acts
> 8:14). They are included because later tradition (Polycrates, Eusebius) blends the two, and the
> Hierapolis tradition may originally belong to the evangelist. The map should not present
> them as the apostle's journeys.

## Competing traditions

### Tradition 1 — Which Philip lived and died at Hierapolis?
- **Claim A: the apostle.** Polycrates (c. 190) explicitly says "Philip, one of the twelve apostles"
  (HE 3.31.3; 5.24.2). Eusebius follows him, calling him "Philip the apostle" (HE 3.39.9), and then
  quotes Acts 21:8–9 about the *evangelist's* daughters as if it were the same man (HE 3.31.5).
  Grade **B**. Defended by J. B. Lightfoot, *Colossians and Philemon* (1875), p. 45 (via McGiffert's note; Lightfoot's page not checked directly — UNVERIFIED page number).
- **Claim B: the evangelist, later confused with the apostle.** Proclus (c. 200) speaks of *four*
  prophetess daughters — exactly the evangelist's family in Acts 21:9. Polycrates' daughters are
  also described as virgins and Spirit-filled. McGiffert (NPNF² vol. 1, note on HE 3.31.3):
  "Philip the apostle and Philip the evangelist are here confounded … That it was the evangelist,
  and not the apostle, that was buried in Hierapolis may be assumed." Grade of the underlying
  claim: **A** for the evangelist's four daughters; **B** for their Hierapolis tomb.
- **Tension:** Clement of Alexandria (c. 200) says Philip "gave his daughters in marriage"
  (Stromata 3.6, in HE 3.30.1), while Polycrates and Acts call them virgins. This is either
  different daughters or another sign the traditions have merged.
- **Scholarly view:** no consensus. Many commentators since McGiffert (1890) think the two were
  conflated, with the evangelist the more likely occupant of the Hierapolis tomb; others follow
  Polycrates. Full treatment: C. R. Matthews, *Philip: Apostle and Evangelist* (Brill, 2002) —
  exists, conclusions not checked here (UNVERIFIED).

### Tradition 2 — Phrygia (and Hierapolis) as mission field
Pseudo-Hippolytus, *On the Twelve Apostles* 5: "Philip preached in Phrygia". Grade C/D: an
anonymous Byzantine-era list printed among Hippolytus' spurious works (ANF 5); date uncertain.
Consistent with Tradition 1 but adds nothing earlier.

### Tradition 3 — Gaul / "the barbarian peoples on the ocean coast"
Isidore of Seville (d. 636), *De ortu et obitu patrum*: Philip "brought the faith to the barbarian
peoples who sat in darkness on the coasts of the ocean … in the end was crucified, stoned … at
Hierapolis … buried between his two daughters" (English as quoted by the Columbia Medieval
Architecture glossary; Golden Legend: "Philip preached to the Frenchmen"). Grade **C** (7th c.,
no earlier source found). Some scholars suspect "Gallis" is a scribal confusion for "Galatis"
(Galatians) — UNVERIFIED, not found in a citable source during this research.
The Latin *Breviarium apostolorum* (c. 6th–7th c.) is reported to say Philip preached to the
Gauls and died at Hierapolis in Phrygia, which may be Isidore's source — UNVERIFIED (Latin text
not found online during this research).

### Tradition 4 — Scythia
*Golden Legend* (c. 1260): preached in Scythia "by the space of twenty years", seized and made to
sacrifice to Mars, freed by expelling a dragon, then went to Hierapolis. Grade **D**.

### Tradition 5 — The apocryphal Acts of Philip
Greek *Acts of Philip* (mid–late 4th c., 15 acts + martyrdom; best manuscript Xenophontos,
Mount Athos, found by F. Bovon 1974): journeys through Athens, Parthia, Azotus, "Nicatera",
ending at Ophioryme ("serpent street" = Hierapolis) with Bartholomew and Mariamne. Encratite
flavour (celibacy, vegetarianism). Grade **D**.

## Open questions / uncertainties

1. **Two Philips.** Is the Hierapolis tomb tradition about the apostle or the evangelist? Polycrates
   (B) says apostle; the four prophetess daughters (Proclus, Acts 21:9) point to the evangelist.
   The map should probably show Hierapolis with an explicit "apostle or evangelist?" caveat.
   Lead to follow up: Oxford *Cult of Saints* E01153, a late-antique ostracon from Hierapolis that
   may mention Philip the deacon/evangelist (not examined).
2. **Vreugdenhil's "bound to a pillar and starved".** No ancient source found. The "pillar" seems to
   come from 17th–18th-c. Protestant compilations (van Braght 1660 — pillar + stoning; Britannica
   1797 — "hanging him up against a pillar", wrongly attributed to Eusebius 3.30). Starvation:
   source unknown. Possibly Vreugdenhil's Dutch original says something slightly different —
   check the Dutch *Kerkgeschiedenis voor jong en oud* wording.
3. **Did Philip die a martyr at all?** The earliest witnesses (Polycrates, Heracleon via Clement)
   do not support a violent death; every martyrdom account is 4th c. or later.
4. **Date of death.** No early source dates it. Only Pseudo-Hippolytus ("time of Domitian",
   81–96, late source), Foxe (AD 54, no basis given), Golden Legend (age 87).
5. **Isidore's "Gauls".** Latin text not verified online; possible Gallia/Galatia confusion is a
   suggestion only (UNVERIFIED).
6. **2011 tomb.** The 1st-c. tomb identified by D'Andria had not been opened at the time of the
   2012 interview; the find proves a 5th-c. cult, not whose body it was.
7. **Pseudo-Hippolytus date.** Commonly treated as a late Byzantine compilation; a precise date
   was not established here.
